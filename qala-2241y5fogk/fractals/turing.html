The plate that divides Gray–Scott reaction–diffusion · the nonlinear step past Chladni

A Chladni plate is linear mode selection: a wavelength is chosen and a figure appears, held still. Add one nonlinear term and a supply of fuel and the same kind of wavelength selection produces form that grows, wanders, and splits in two. How many frequencies would it take to build a cell that divides? None — a cell is not a standing wave. This is what lives one step across that boundary: two abstract concentrations, u eaten by v, v dying at rate F+k, fed at rate F. Nothing here is alive.

Sound — a stated mapping, not physics

f = S / λ with S = 5760 Hz·cells — the same shape as a plate mode’s wavelength→pitch map, with the scale factor stated. Patterns do not hum; silence when no wavelength is confident.

Chladni feed — the sound decides where the chemistry runs

F(x, y) = F ± depth · sin(mπx/L)sin(nπy/L) — a standing-wave plate mode (simply-supported idealisation, as Qālā states) modulating the feed. Where the mode is strong the chemistry is fed differently; the plate’s geometry chooses where, Gray–Scott chooses what. The map shows the F-range as a vertical bar.

What is NOT modelled

  • No chemistry. u and v are abstract concentrations; no real reagents behave exactly like this.
  • No DNA, no membrane, no metabolism. The spots divide because of diffusion and the one nonlinear term uv² — not because they are alive.
  • Why it looks alive: it is a dissipative structure — form that persists by consuming a feed, like a flame. Staying alive is energy flux, not frequency count.
  • The dish has no walls. Periodic boundaries, chosen so the FFT measurement is exact; a real dish reflects at its rim.
  • Discretisation stated: 256² grid, explicit Euler, dt = 1 under the stability bound 1.192; Du = 2·10⁻⁵, Dv = 10⁻⁵, L = 2.5 (Pearson 1993).
