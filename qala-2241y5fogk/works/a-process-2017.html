Musrara Mix Festival 17  ·  Jerusalem  ·  7 June 2017

A Process

A live performance for guitar, no-input mixer, live image — and a plate of charcoal standing open to the audience. Thirty-seven minutes of one continuous feedback loop, in which matter listens and the room plays.

See Qālā, the instrument this became
I  ·  The first sound

The universe rang before it shone.

For its first 380,000 years, the universe was a sea of plasma so dense that light could not cross it. Every photon was scattered the instant it was born. In that era there was no seeing — but there was hearing.

Pressure waves — sound, in the most literal physical sense — rolled through the primordial plasma at over half the speed of light. The whole cosmos was a resonating body: a single medium, ringing. When the universe finally cooled enough for atoms to form, the fog lifted, light streamed free for the first time — and the sound froze mid-vibration. Wherever a wave happened to be, there it stopped, its crest written into the density of matter itself.

Those frozen crests are still legible in the sky. Galaxies today cluster preferentially at one particular separation — the distance the primordial sound managed to travel before it was silenced. Cosmologists call it the sound horizon. It is, plainly said, the wavelength of the first sound, and the galaxies are its ripples.

380,000 yrthe age of sound before light — the era of the opaque, ringing universe
~500 M lythe sound horizon today — the frozen wavelength written into galaxy clustering
× 10²⁶the pitch-shift needed to bring the sound of the early universe into human hearing

The first light of the universe — the cosmic microwave background — is therefore not a picture of light at all. Its mottled pattern is the interference pattern of those pressure waves at the moment they stopped. The oldest photograph in existence is a photograph of sound. Physicists have even resynthesized it: shifted up some twenty-six orders of magnitude, the early universe plays as a deep, descending roar.

This is the parallel the performance draws. Light is how the universe is seen; sound is how it was shaped. One consistent wave, breaking into the vibrational ripples we now call matter, structure, life.

II  ·  Sound shapes matter

What the cosmos did to galaxies, a tone does to charcoal.

Scatter grains on a plate, set it vibrating, and matter leaps into geometry — nodal lines, mandalas, lattices. Ernst Chladni showed it in 1787; Hans Jenny filmed it and named the field cymatics. Vibration does not decorate matter. It organizes it.

This is the engine of the performance — and it was built by hand. In the auditorium stands a custom tectonic plate: a speaker turned instrument, its surface dressed with charcoal. Sound driven through the plate makes the charcoal leap, drift, and settle into pattern; a camera mounted above sends the living surface to the video artist, who fuses it with the sound itself and returns it to the screen, monumentally enlarged. And the plate is not fenced off — the audience is free to approach it, touch it, disturb it. Every disturbance enters the image.

The choice of dust is not arbitrary. Charcoal is carbon — the building block itself: forged inside stars that condensed along the ripples of the first sound, the skeleton of every living molecule, ground down to graphite black. And that black takes a position on light. Charcoal neither reflects the beam nor releases it — light that lands on it ends there, unanswered. On a stage made entirely of projection, it is the one material that refuses the play of light. Only sound can move it. So on the plate, the oldest process in the universe repeats at arm's length: vibration teaching matter its shape.

Nothing on the screen is an animation. It is physics, performed.

III  ·  The performance

A black stage. A screen the size of the sky.

Musrara, Jerusalem — 7 June 2017, nine o'clock at night. In the Musrara Mix Festival's electro-acoustic program the piece was billed simply 1+1+1: three artists inside one circuit — a guitar, a mixer turned on itself, a camera over a plate of charcoal — and a stage given over to a single luminous rectangle.

The sound begins as almost nothing — static, hum, the electricity of the room. A guitarist bends a single note through an array of effects and loopers. A second artist receives that sound into a no-input mixer — a mixing desk fed back on itself, feeding on its own electrical interference — where note and noise breed. The sound drives the tectonic plate; the plate draws in charcoal; the camera lifts the drawing to the video artist, who fuses image with sound and pours both onto the screen. The light of the screen falls back on the room, the room leans in on the plate — audience, interference, charcoal, guitar, light — all in, all in a loop of feedback, all in the creation together.

The piece unfolds exactly as its name promises. From a static, to the single bend of a note, to a cluster, a formation, a composition — where once there was nothing but raw material, out comes a pouring of light and sound, of life and motion, of emotion and experience. Once, near the middle, the field floods with violet — the only colour in the work — like first light arriving in a universe that had only ever heard itself.

Then the process reverses. Forms dissolve, the field quiets, the screen dims toward the black it came from. But a feedback loop has no final bar: A Process is the beginning and the end at the same time — a superposition, a state held within a state.

IV  ·  Continuation

The ripple did not stop here.

The performance ended in 2017. Its wave kept traveling.

Out of this event, its guest artist built a new instrument: Qālā — the plate, the no-input mixer, and the fusion of image and sound, rebuilt as one machine anyone can play. It is a separate work, by one pair of hands, that carries this evening's DNA. The wave at the head of this page is still running; it has been running since you arrived.

You, reading, are standing where the audience stood.
